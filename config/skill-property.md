# Property — Domain-Specific Financial Model Review Skill

This file contains **property-domain knowledge only**: the physical, legal-economic,
market, development, leasing, valuation and property-investment relationships that a
property professional should understand when reviewing a financial model. Generic
spreadsheet review, audit evidence, severity, reporting, governance, formula engineering,
generic project-finance procedures and general financial-statement controls belong to the
core audit infrastructure and are intentionally not repeated here.

The current version cumulatively incorporates durable principles from A. F. Millington,
*An Introduction to Property Valuation* (3rd ed., 1988), Roger Staiger, *Foundations of Real
Estate Financial Modelling* (3rd ed.), Michael Blackledge, *Introducing Property Valuation*
(2nd ed., 2017), Lucy Ogbenjuwa, *Real Estate Finance and Economics* (2023), and Piyush Tiwari
and Michael White, *Real Estate Finance in the New Economy* (2014). It has also been benchmarked
against current international property standards and primary/peer-reviewed research available in
August 2026, including RICS Red Book Global Standards 2025, current IVS, RICS development-property,
DCF, comparable-evidence, measurement, cost-prediction, technical-due-diligence, service-charge and
ESG/sustainability materials, and peer-reviewed real-estate research on development optionality,
property depreciation, appraisal evidence and sustainability pricing.

Historical tax rules, market yields, legal conventions, statutory references, teaching-case percentages,
country-specific financing rankings, survey percentages, historical cost/return benchmarks and numerical
examples are **not** universal requirements. Durable property-economic relationships are retained;
jurisdiction-, asset-, transaction- and valuation-date-specific assumptions must be supported by the
applicable current framework and market evidence.

## Model type

Apply to real estate and property-development models including:

- build-to-sell residential, commercial and mixed-use developments;
- land subdivision and staged masterplanned communities;
- build-to-rent and other income-producing property;
- property investment / hold models;
- property trusts or asset-holding vehicles where asset-level property economics drive value;
- hospitality, leisure, entertainment and mixed-use assets where a property development is
  followed by an operating phase;
- hold-versus-sell and redevelopment-versus-existing-use decision models.

Classify the case before applying the checks. At minimum distinguish:

1. **Development / build-to-sell** — land and development expenditure are converted into
   completed sale proceeds.
2. **Income-producing / investment property** — value is driven primarily by net property
   income, lease terms, market rent, outgoings, yield/capitalisation or DCF.
3. **Develop-and-operate** — the project contains both a development phase and a continuing
   operating asset/business phase.
4. **Redevelopment / alternative-use** — value depends on releasing latent site value through
   refurbishment, change of use, intensification or redevelopment.

Do not force every property model into a single generic template. Property is heterogeneous;
two apparently similar assets can have materially different value because of location, tenure,
lease terms, condition, planning rights, access, services, configuration or market demand.

## Property is an interest, not just a building

A financial model should represent the **economic interest actually owned or being acquired**,
not merely the physical structure. Depending on the jurisdiction and transaction, economically
material interests or constraints can include:

- freehold / fee-simple ownership;
- leasehold interests and remaining lease term;
- headlease, sublease and profit-rent positions;
- easements, rights of way, rights of light or access rights;
- restrictive covenants or use restrictions;
- repair, maintenance, insurance and service-charge obligations;
- options, break rights, renewal rights or reversionary interests;
- planning/development rights and conditions;
- ownership or title limitations that affect transferability or use.

Where these features affect rent, marketability, redevelopment, holding cost or terminal value,
the model should reflect their economics. Do not assume that two physically identical buildings
have equal value if the legal/economic interests differ.

## Valuation purpose and valuation date

Property value is purpose- and date-specific. A model should make clear what economic question
its valuation is answering, for example:

- acquisition or sale price;
- development-site value;
- completed development value / GDV;
- investment value of an income-producing asset;
- market rent / rental value;
- hold-versus-sell value;
- redevelopment value;
- lender or security analysis;
- insurance/replacement-cost analysis;
- internal investment appraisal.

Do not mix valuation purposes. Replacement cost, market value, investment value, development
residual and insurance/reinstatement value can legitimately produce different answers because
they answer different questions.

The valuation date must also be coherent with the market evidence, rent assumptions, interest
rates, cost base and planning/legal position used. Property markets change over time; stale
transaction evidence is not automatically valid merely because the asset appears similar.

### Current property valuation standards and status discipline

For a model that uses or claims alignment with a valuation standard, identify the **operative** standard
for the valuation date, jurisdiction, purpose and valuer. As at August 2026:

- RICS Valuation – Global Standards effective 31 January 2025 is the current global Red Book;
- the current IVS was published in 2024 and became effective 31 January 2025;
- IVS 400 covers Real Property Interests and IVS 410 covers Development Property in the current edition;
- the IVS Exposure Draft issued in 2026 is **not** operative; it is proposed for a later edition;
- RICS ESG and sustainability in commercial property valuation, 4th edition, is effective from
  30 April 2026;
- RICS Service charges in commercial property, 2nd edition, is effective from 31 December 2025;
- RICS Code of Measuring Practice 6th edition and/or IPMS: All Buildings remain the current measurement
  references where applicable while the proposed 7th edition is still under consultation; and
- RICS Comparable evidence in real estate valuation 1st edition remains the published reference while
  the proposed 2nd edition is still under consultation in 2026.

Local law, regulation, contractual definitions and national valuation standards can have superior or
additional authority. Do not convert a consultation draft, exposure draft, archived edition or another
jurisdiction's convention into a binding model criterion merely because it is newer or more detailed.

### Value uncertainty is not the same as a valuation defect

Property is heterogeneous, transactions can be infrequent and development/residual valuations can be
highly sensitive. Different reasonable evidence, assumptions or methods may therefore support a range of
credible values even when each is competently applied. Treat this **value uncertainty** separately from an
actual modelling or valuation defect.

Where uncertainty is material, the model should make the economic sources visible — for example thin
comparable evidence, uncertain letting velocity, planning outcome, construction cost, exit yield or
redevelopment timing — rather than manufacturing false precision in a single point estimate. A valuation
range or scenario set is not automatically superior to a point estimate; it is useful when it reflects genuine
property-market uncertainty and is tied to supportable assumptions.

### Price, market value, investment value/worth and cost are different

Keep the economic concepts separate:

- **price** is the amount actually asked, offered or paid in a particular transaction;
- **market value** is an estimate of the price likely to arise under the stated market-value basis;
- **investment value / worth** is the value of the property to a particular owner or prospective
  owner given that party's required return, financing, tax position, synergies or strategic use;
- **cost** is expenditure incurred or estimated to acquire, build or replace an asset and is not
  automatically evidence of value.

A transaction price can differ from market value, and a rational investor-specific worth can differ
from market value without either figure being arithmetically wrong. When a model claims a market
value, do not let buyer-specific financing, tax, adjoining ownership or strategic benefits silently
enter the market-value case. Show such benefits separately where they are decision-relevant.

### Special / synergistic value — conditional

Combining interests or parcels can create value that does not exist in the standalone interests.
Examples include adjoining sites that unlock a larger development envelope or the merger of a
leasehold and freehold interest that removes an encumbrance. Where this occurs:

- value each standalone interest on a coherent basis;
- identify the incremental value created by combination;
- identify which buyer(s) can actually realise that increment; and
- avoid counting the synergy once in the standalone values and again as a separate premium.

Do not assume special/synergistic value is part of general market value merely because one specific
buyer can realise it.

## Property-market characteristics that affect financial models

### Heterogeneity and imperfect comparability

Property is heterogeneous and local. Comparable evidence should be normalised for economically
material differences rather than applied mechanically. Relevant adjustments can include:

- micro-location and access;
- site area and usable/net lettable area;
- frontage, configuration, floorplate and layout;
- condition, age, refurbishment and obsolescence;
- quality of construction and specification;
- car parking, loading, storage and ancillary space;
- tenure and lease structure;
- permitted use and planning status;
- services, utilities and infrastructure;
- tenant quality and lease covenant where investment property is being compared;
- sale conditions, transaction timing and unusual purchaser/seller motivations.

A comparison based only on $/sqm or $/unit is weak where these factors differ materially.

Comparable evidence should be **weighted**, not mechanically averaged. Recent arm's-length
transactions in the same relevant market are often strong evidence, but asking prices, bids, marketing
history, distressed sales, indices and other data can still be informative when interpreted in context.
A distressed transaction is not automatically unusable; identify the distress and adjust for its effect where
supportable. Likewise, later/post-dated evidence should not be back-cast automatically: use it only where it
helps illuminate conditions that existed at the valuation date and where hindsight is not being smuggled into
the value conclusion.

### Measurement basis and floor-area discipline

Property models commonly use more than one area definition. The economically correct denominator
depends on the item being modelled. For example, construction cost may be quoted per gross/built
area, rent per lettable area, residential sales per saleable area and planning density on another
gross-floor or site-area basis.

Where multiple area bases exist, maintain an explicit bridge between them and make each rate's
denominator clear. Challenge cases where:

- a construction cost rate per gross area is applied to net lettable/saleable area;
- rent or sale evidence per net/saleable area is multiplied by gross built area;
- efficiency ratios between gross, net and saleable area are inconsistent with the design;
- car parking, balconies, storage, common areas or ancillary space are counted inconsistently; or
- a historical/local measurement convention is silently treated as identical to a different current
  standard.

Do not hard-code one historical measurement standard as universal. Use the measurement basis
applicable to the asset, jurisdiction and market evidence, and make conversions explicit where they
affect value or cost.

#### Land ownership area, planning site area and net development area

For development land, do not use the word **site** as though every land-area measure were identical.
Where relevant, distinguish at least:

- the legal land ownership/title area;
- the site area used for planning/development assessment; and
- the net development area actually available for buildings or development parcels after roads,
  open space, setbacks, easements, reserves, infrastructure or other exclusions.

Density, land price per area, infrastructure allocation, site coverage and GDV should use the area basis
appropriate to that metric. A change in gross/title area does not necessarily create an equal change in
net developable or saleable area.

### Retail zoning / ITZA — conditional

In retail markets where comparable rents are analysed using zoning, ITZA or another frontage/depth
convention, total floor area is not treated as economically homogeneous. Frontage and depth can
change rental value materially. The model should use the **same measurement and zoning basis as the
market evidence** and should not compare a zoned rent with an unzoned whole-area rate as if they were
equivalent.

Historical zone depths and halving-back conventions are market conventions, not universal rules.
Apply zoning only where current local evidence and property type support it.

### Location and accessibility

Location can dominate property economics. Modelled rents, prices, occupancy and terminal values
should be consistent with the asset's actual location characteristics, including where relevant:

- transport accessibility and travel time;
- proximity to employment, retail, education, amenities or customers;
- road, rail, port or airport access for commercial/industrial assets;
- catchment area and passing trade for retail/leisure uses;
- surrounding uses and externalities;
- topography, aspect and site constraints;
- local infrastructure and availability of services.

A model should not apply prime-location pricing to secondary property merely because gross area
or building type is similar.

### Planning and permissible use

Planning/development rights can create or destroy substantial land value. Where development,
change of use, density, height, staging or subdivision depends on planning approval, distinguish:

- existing lawful use;
- approved development rights;
- conditional or staged approvals;
- applications not yet determined;
- aspirational higher-use assumptions with no current entitlement.

Do not capitalise an unapproved higher use with the same certainty as an approved scheme unless
the model explicitly probability-weights or otherwise recognises approval risk.

### Physical condition, adaptability and obsolescence

An asset's value depends not only on age but on economic usefulness. Relevant factors can include:

- structural/physical condition;
- deferred maintenance;
- functional obsolescence;
- inefficient floorplate or access;
- inability to accommodate modern equipment or tenant requirements;
- energy/building-services limitations;
- adaptability to alternative use;
- expansion potential;
- refurbishment requirements.

For specialist or older property, replacement cost should not be treated as market value without
allowing for depreciation, obsolescence and whether the market actually values the specialised
improvements.

Physical obsolescence can also be accelerated by regulatory, environmental or occupier-market
requirements. Where material, consider whether energy performance, flood exposure, building
services, accessibility, climate resilience or other sustainability-related characteristics require
capital expenditure, reduce lettability/rent, shorten economic life or impair saleability. Do not
import a dated statutory threshold from a textbook; model the current requirement and market effect
applicable to the property.

## Property valuation approaches

Use the valuation approach that matches the asset, available evidence and economic question.
Multiple approaches can be useful as cross-checks where more than one is supportable.

### 1. Comparable / market approach

Use recent market evidence from genuinely comparable transactions or lettings. The review should
challenge:

- whether the transactions are sufficiently recent for current market conditions;
- whether the comparison unit is appropriate (e.g. $/sqm, $/lot, $/room, $/unit, rent per sqm);
- whether location, condition, specification, tenure and transaction terms are comparable;
- whether the model has adjusted for material differences rather than simply averaging prices;
- whether unusual or compelled transactions have been treated as normal market evidence.

Historical evidence can become misleading when financing conditions, supply/demand, planning,
interest rates or investor preferences have changed materially.

Comparable evidence should be **weighted by relevance and reliability**, not mechanically averaged.
Record enough transaction context to understand whether the evidence is genuinely market-derived.
For rent evidence in particular, a recent arm's-length letting of the subject or a closely comparable
property may be stronger evidence than an inferred rent, while actual passing rent can be weak
evidence of current market rent if it is stale, related-party, bundled with a premium/goodwill, or part
of a wider sale-and-leaseback or other transaction.

Where the model infers rent from capital transactions, profits/turnover, construction cost or an
occupier-affordability approach, make the conversion basis explicit and recognise that these are not
automatically equivalent in evidential strength to a recent arm's-length letting.

### 2. Cost / contractor / replacement-cost approach

A cost-based method can be relevant where market transactions are scarce, especially for
special-purpose property. Conceptually:

`Indicative property value = land/site value + replacement cost of improvements - depreciation - obsolescence`

Cost is not automatically value. Challenge any model that equates construction spend with market
value without considering depreciation, functional/economic obsolescence and market utility.

### 3. Residual / development approach

For land or redevelopment opportunities, the core relationship is:

`Residual land/site value = completed development value - all development costs - required developer return`

The completed-development value is often referred to as GDV / gross realisation. Relevant costs
can include, where applicable:

- acquisition costs and transaction taxes;
- demolition/site preparation;
- hard construction cost;
- professional and consultant fees;
- authority contributions and infrastructure;
- contingency;
- financing/holding costs;
- marketing, sales and leasing costs;
- taxes/duties specifically attributable to development;
- developer's required profit/return.

Residual value is highly sensitive because the land value is the **residual after large inflows and
outflows**. Small percentage changes in GDV, build cost, programme or finance cost can cause a
large percentage change in land value. A model should therefore not present residual value with
false precision or without testing its principal property drivers.

Before accepting the residual, confirm the proposed development is physically and legally
plausible for the site and that it represents the modelled development alternative, rather than an
unsupported assumption about the highest-value use.

Where credible evidence exists, cross-check the residual site value against actual market evidence
for comparable development opportunities or another supportable site-value indicator. A residual is
particularly vulnerable to small input errors because land value is the balancing amount after much
larger inflows and outflows. Do not turn a textbook site-value-as-a-percentage-of-GDV check into a
universal threshold; use it only as a market-context reasonableness check where comparable evidence
supports the relationship.

### 4. Profits / trading-potential approach — conditional

For properties whose value is inseparable from trading potential and where direct comparable
property evidence is weak — historically including hotels, pubs, restaurants, leisure and other
specialist trading properties — the property model may need to distinguish:

- maintainable gross receipts;
- operating purchases and expenses;
- a fair return/remuneration for the operator's labour, capital and business risk;
- the residual amount attributable to occupation of the property.

Do not capitalise exceptional operator performance as if it were automatically property rent. A
highly skilled or unusually poor operator can distort observed trading results relative to the
maintainable earnings of the property itself.

### 5. Investment / income capitalisation approach

For income-producing property, value is linked to the net property income and the return required
by the market. The review should understand:

- passing/current rent;
- market/full rental value;
- rent-free periods and incentives;
- lease expiry and review dates;
- vacancy and downtime;
- landlord versus tenant outgoings;
- repairs, insurance, service charges and non-recoverable expenditure;
- tenant covenant/credit and concentration risk where relevant;
- reversionary income after current lease terms expire;
- the yield/capitalisation rate selected and what risks it reflects.

A lower required yield implies a higher capital value, all else equal. Do not accept a yield merely
because it is labelled "market"; its relationship to asset quality, lease security, location,
obsolescence and current transaction evidence should be supportable.

#### Property yield taxonomy and growth basis

Do not treat every quoted property yield as interchangeable. Depending on the market convention and
analysis, distinguish where relevant:

- **initial yield** — current/passing net income relative to price/value;
- **reversionary yield** — a yield relationship using market/reversionary income rather than only
  current passing income;
- **equivalent yield** — a single yield/IRR consistent with a traditional term-and-reversion or
  layer analysis in which expected growth is largely implicit in the market yield; and
- **equated yield / explicit-growth return** — a target return used with an explicit forecast of
  rental/capital growth in a DCF.

The labels used by markets can vary. The durable discipline is to understand what income, timing,
growth and risk assumptions are embedded in the quoted yield before applying it. A model can
double count growth if it applies explicit rental/capital growth and also uses a yield calibrated on a
basis that already embeds the same growth expectation without a coherent conversion.

#### Income timing and yield convention

Property rents can be paid monthly, quarterly, half-yearly or annually, and in advance or arrears.
Yield evidence and traditional capitalisation formulae can also be quoted on nominal or effective
bases. Where timing is material, ensure the modelled cash-flow timing and the market yield
convention are consistent. Do not apply an annually-in-arrears capitalisation factor to quarterly-in-
advance income as though the timing were identical.

### Exit / reversion value from forward property income

For an income-producing property sold at the end of period `n`, the capitalised income should
represent the income an incoming buyer is purchasing — ordinarily a supportable forward or
next-period property NOI, not an arbitrary same-period monthly run-rate and not net income after
debt service. If the model derives forward NOI by escalating the final operating year, check that
the underlying year is representative and that one unusually strong month has not been annualised
into terminal value.

Keep the property-value and equity-proceeds bridge separate:

`gross property sale value - disposal costs = net property sale proceeds`

`net property sale proceeds - debt/other financing settled at exit = residual equity sale proceeds`

Debt repayment changes equity proceeds; it does not change the underlying property value generated
from property income and market yield.

### Passing rent versus market rent and reversion

Where passing rent differs materially from market rent, the model should not simply capitalise one
number forever. It may need to distinguish:

- current contracted income for the remaining term;
- rent review mechanics;
- expiry/break and reletting assumptions;
- market rent on reversion;
- lease incentives, downtime and leasing costs;
- outgoings during vacancy.

The timing of the reversion matters. A market-rent uplift that cannot legally or commercially occur
for several years should not be recognised as immediate income.

#### Over-rented property and reverse-premium economics

Where passing rent is above current market rent, treat the excess rent as a distinct, finite and
credit-sensitive cash flow rather than simply capitalising it as superior perpetual income. For the
landlord, above-market rent can increase near-term income but also increase exposure to tenant
default because re-letting after failure may occur only at the lower market rent and may require
downtime, repair and leasing costs.

For a tenant holding an onerous lease, the above-market rent can make the leasehold interest a
liability. Assignment or surrender may require a **reverse premium** or other compensation. The
model should recognise the remaining loss-rent period, break/review/expiry dates and any costs of
exiting or restructuring the lease.

### Leasehold / terminable interests

A leasehold interest can be a wasting asset because the right to receive/use the property expires.
Where leasehold economics are material, value should respond to:

- unexpired term;
- rent payable under the headlease;
- market rent receivable from subletting;
- review pattern;
- subletting/assignment rights;
- obligations and restrictions;
- residual/reversionary rights at expiry.

Do not value a short or restricted leasehold as though it were economically equivalent to freehold
ownership.

### 6. Discounted cash flow

DCF is especially useful when property cash flows vary by year because it can reflect explicit
period-by-period rents, vacancy, incentives, capex, outgoings, redevelopment costs and exit value.
The property-specific discipline is to forecast **net cash flows at the time they actually arise**
and discount them at a rate consistent with the risk and cash-flow basis. Monthly settlements,
lease events, development draws, recapitalisations and irregular distributions should not be
silently treated as though they all occur at equal annual intervals where timing is material.

The quality of a DCF is determined by the quality of its property assumptions. A mathematically
precise DCF built from unsupported rents, occupancy, lease expiry, capex or terminal value is not a
reliable property valuation.

Property cash flows can change sign more than once because of acquisition, later refurbishment,
additional equity calls, recapitalisation or subsequent capital expenditure. In that case a single
IRR can be ambiguous or economically misleading. Use NPV/date-based cash-flow analysis as a
primary anchor and explain any modified return measure rather than presenting one IRR as
unquestioned truth.

Where the model uses both direct capitalisation and DCF, reconcile the implied assumptions. A DCF
whose terminal capitalisation rate and stabilised NOI imply a materially different value from the
standalone capitalisation case should explain the difference.

#### Explicit forecast period and exit-value reliance

Choose the explicit DCF period for a property reason, not because a template happens to contain a fixed
number of years. Depending on the asset, a sensible horizon can be linked to lease expiries, a major
refurbishment/redevelopment decision, stabilisation or a sufficiently complete property-market cycle.

The terminal/exit value is commonly one of the largest components of investment-property value. Reconcile
it to the **forward** income and property condition at the exit date, not the current NOI by default. Where a
large share of value sits in the terminal value, challenge whether the assumed exit rent, vacancy, capex,
remaining economic life and capitalisation rate are mutually consistent.

#### Location growth versus property-specific rental depreciation

Market or location-level rental growth is not automatically the rental growth of an ageing building. An
asset can participate in market growth while simultaneously losing relative rent through physical
deterioration, functional obsolescence, inferior specification or changing occupier requirements.

Where material, separate:

- market/location rental growth;
- property-specific rental depreciation or obsolescence; and
- any reset in relative quality caused by refurbishment/repositioning capex.

Do not hard-code one annual depreciation rate. Peer-reviewed evidence indicates that depreciation varies
by asset quality, age, market and supply conditions. The exit yield may also move as a building ages or
becomes more/less competitive; assuming an unchanged prime exit yield while the property deteriorates
requires specific support.

### NPV versus IRR in property investment decisions

NPV answers whether projected property cash flows create value at a stated target return; IRR is
the discount rate that makes NPV zero. For mutually exclusive property alternatives, the model
should not automatically prefer the option with the highest IRR if another option generates more
NPV at the investor's required return.

## Development economics

### Total development cost

Total development cost should reflect the full economic cost of creating and realising the
property. Depending on the project this may include:

- land purchase and acquisition costs;
- demolition/remediation/site works;
- building works;
- professional fees;
- statutory/authority charges;
- infrastructure and utilities;
- contingency;
- financing and holding costs;
- marketing/leasing/sales costs;
- applicable property-specific taxes/duties;
- fitout/furniture/equipment where needed to achieve the modelled sale/rent;
- operating deficits or working capital before stabilisation where the asset is retained.

Do not assume a single universal cost taxonomy. The key property principle is that costs required
to achieve the modelled completed value must not disappear outside the development appraisal.

#### Cost-prediction basis and cost-to-complete

A development cost forecast should be tied to a defined scheme, measurement basis, design maturity,
price/base date and scope. Rates from a different gross/net area basis, specification, location, procurement
route or date should be normalised before use. Keep cost classification sufficiently consistent that land,
construction, external works, infrastructure, professional fees, statutory costs, fit-out, contingency and
other material development components cannot migrate between categories and disappear.

For a scheme already under construction, the economically relevant question is **cost to complete**, not
only original budget. Reconcile, where applicable:

- work completed/certified and paid to date;
- committed but unpaid construction/professional costs;
- remaining works and revised quantities;
- variations, claims and known defects/remedial work;
- remaining contingency and whether it is still available for unknown risk;
- retained sums, warranties and completion obligations; and
- timing of the remaining spend against the completion programme.

A project can remain within original headline budget while becoming underfunded if contingency has been
consumed early or remaining obligations are understated.

### Development timing

Land acquisition, design, approvals, construction, practical completion, leasing/sales and
settlement occur at different dates. Property value and financing costs are timing-sensitive.
The model should distinguish where material:

- deposit/exchange and land settlement;
- planning and design periods;
- staged construction;
- practical completion;
- defects/commissioning period;
- presales or preleasing;
- settlement/tenant commencement;
- stabilisation;
- disposal/refinancing.

Do not assume that practical completion, sale contract, settlement and cash receipt are the same
event.

#### Entitlement as a separate development stage and real option

Planning/entitlement and construction are not always one indivisible decision. Securing or advancing an
entitlement can create value by reducing legal/planning uncertainty while preserving the option to delay,
redesign, phase, sell or later construct. Conversely, waiting has value only where the owner can actually
wait without losing tenure, approvals, option rights, market position or incurring disproportionate holding
costs and obligations.

Where optionality is material, distinguish the economics of:

1. acquiring/holding the land or development right;
2. obtaining or improving entitlement; and
3. committing irreversible construction capital.

Do not assume the highest density or immediate construction is automatically optimal solely because it
maximises a static GDV.

#### Development in progress / partially completed schemes

A partly completed development is not simply the completed GDV less the original unspent budget.
Value can depend on the exact stage reached and the rights/risks transferred. Consider, where relevant:

- works completed and remaining;
- contractor/professional appointments and ability to novate or replace them;
- outstanding claims, variations and disputes;
- warranties, bonds/guarantees and latent-defect protection;
- planning or consent conditions still to be discharged;
- pre-sales/pre-lets and their continuing enforceability;
- site security, deterioration and remobilisation cost if work pauses; and
- the purchaser's time and risk to finish the scheme.

Market evidence for completed assets or bare land can therefore be a weak proxy for a unique work-in-
progress interest unless the bridge is explicit.

### GDV / completed-development value

For build-to-sell projects, GDV should reconcile to the actual inventory of saleable lots/units/areas
and the modelled price basis. For hold projects, completed value should reconcile to the income or
other valuation method actually used.

Where the model uses both development margin and a later investment valuation, distinguish:

- value on completion;
- stabilised value after lease-up/operations;
- final exit value at a later date.

These can be different economic points in time and should not be conflated.

### Developer return

Developer profit/return is compensation for time, capital, execution risk and market risk; it is not
merely an arithmetic plug. The required return should fit the project's risk, duration and market
context. Do not convert historical textbook percentages into a universal hurdle.

#### Residual output: land value versus development return

Residual appraisal can solve different economic questions. If completed value, development costs and
required developer return are inputs, the residual may represent a land/site value. If land cost is already
known, the same development cash flow can instead solve for developer profit, margin, IRR or another
return measure. Make the residual **output** explicit.

Do not present a residual land value as developer profit, or include both a target developer return and a
second unexplained profit allowance in costs. For phased schemes, ensure the timing of land payments,
construction and receipts is reflected rather than relying on a static arithmetic residual where timing is
material.

### Highest/best development alternative

Where more than one development or refurbishment option is plausible, compare alternatives on a
common valuation date and economic basis. Consider:

- permissible use and planning risk;
- buildable/saleable/lettable area;
- construction and infrastructure burden;
- programme duration;
- achievable market value or rent;
- absorption/lease-up;
- financing and holding cost;
- residual land value / NPV.

A larger scheme is not automatically more valuable if added density materially increases cost,
delay, infrastructure burden or market absorption risk.

## Income-producing property

### Net property income and outgoings

The income to be capitalised or discounted should reflect the economic cash flow of the property
interest being valued. Distinguish:

- gross contracted rent;
- rent concessions/incentives;
- vacancy and bad debt;
- recoverable operating expenses;
- non-recoverable landlord expenses;
- property management costs;
- maintenance and lifecycle capex where relevant;
- statutory charges and insurance where borne by the owner.

Do not capitalise gross rent at a net-income yield or mix gross and net bases across comparable
evidence.

### Yield and risk

Property yield/capitalisation rate is an economic expression of price relative to income and the
market's assessment of risk. Relevant property-specific drivers can include:

- location and asset quality;
- tenant/occupier covenant;
- lease length and break structure;
- passing versus market rent;
- vacancy and reletting risk;
- building age/obsolescence;
- capex burden;
- liquidity and lot size;
- expected rental growth;
- current investment-market conditions.

Do not infer that a higher yield is automatically "better". Higher yield often reflects higher risk or
weaker expected growth.

### Lease incentives and effective rent

Headline rent is not necessarily the economic rent. Lease incentives can include rent-free periods,
stepped rents, cash or fit-out contributions, service-charge caps, relocation support, landlord
take-back of existing premises, break options and other concessions. Where such incentives are
material:

- identify which payments are true inducements versus consideration for tenant works or other
  obligations;
- convert the transaction to a supportable **effective-rent** basis for comparison;
- use a write-off/analysis period consistent with the lease economics and market evidence rather
  than mechanically using the full lease term; and
- do not compare an incentivised headline rent directly with a no-incentive comparable.

### Abnormal rent-review, break and lease-term structure

Lease review frequency, break rights and lease length affect rent and value. If a subject lease has a
materially different review/break pattern from the comparable evidence, normalise the economics or
explain why no adjustment is warranted. Do not import a historical formula, fixed percentage or
"normal" review period as universal practice; use current local market evidence.

### Turnover / percentage rent — conditional

Where rent is linked to tenant turnover or trading receipts, model the contractual rent base rather
than treating all sales as property rent. Relevant terms can include base/minimum rent, turnover
percentage, thresholds, exclusions, caps/floors, reporting periods and the definition of gross versus
net sales. If turnover rent is used as market-rent evidence, distinguish property earning capacity
from operator-specific performance and unusual trading periods.

### Rent roll, rollover and economic vacancy

For multi-unit leased property, aggregate rent should be traceable to the unit/tenant lease
economics when those details drive value. Relevant fields can include unit/tenant identity, area,
lease start/expiry, passing rent, reviews/escalation, abatements, recoveries, market rent, renewal
options and vacancy/downtime.

Distinguish **physical vacancy** from **economic vacancy**. A market report showing a physical
vacancy percentage is not automatically evidence that the model should deduct the same percentage
from gross rent. Economic vacancy can arise from lease-up, rollover timing, downtime, concessions,
bad debt, lost expense recoveries, tenant improvements and leasing commissions. Where lease-level
cash-flow disrupters are modelled, do not add a second blanket vacancy haircut for the same loss
unless it represents a genuinely different risk.

A major lease expiry should be challenged for the full owner economics, including where relevant:

- rent lost during downtime;
- loss of recoveries/service charges;
- market-rent reset;
- tenant improvements/fit-out;
- leasing commissions;
- renewal probability and tenant retention.

Several tenants do not automatically create diversification if they share the same expiry date,
industry, guarantor weakness or local demand driver. For a major tenant, a flat bad-debt percentage
is not a substitute for understanding how tenant business/credit weakness would affect rent,
downtime, reletting cost and property value.

### Recoveries / CAM / service charges

Where tenants reimburse property expenses, reconcile the recoverable expense base with the lease
recovery mechanism. Do not treat recoveries as free standalone revenue detached from the costs and
lease provisions that create them. In office and retail property especially, recoveries may depend on
occupied area, tenant share, caps/floors, gross-versus-net lease terms and vacancy leakage.

Recoverability and **cash timing** are also different. Major works may be funded through retained
service-charge balances, forward funding, later recovery or payment plans depending on the lease and
applicable regime. The owner may therefore face a temporary funding requirement even where a cost is
ultimately recoverable. Do not recognise recovery earlier than the contractual/property mechanism permits,
or assume every capital/lifecycle item is recoverable merely because it benefits the building.

### Seasonality and double-counting

Where seasonal occupancy/rent patterns are relevant, monthly seasonality can be more informative
than a flat annual run-rate. Avoid double counting: if a seasonal profile already embeds the
expected occupancy/vacancy pattern, do not automatically apply a second generic vacancy deduction
to the same revenue.

## Develop-and-operate / mixed property-operating assets

Where a property is developed and then retained as an operating venue, hospitality, leisure or
mixed-use business, keep the property and operating-business economics distinct where necessary.

The model may require two value lenses:

1. **Property / real estate value** — market rent, NOI and property yield/cap rate or property DCF.
2. **Operating business value** — maintainable business earnings, business DCF or an appropriate
   operating multiple.

Avoid double counting. If operating EBITDA includes an implicit economic return for occupying the
property, adding a full capitalised property value on top can overstate enterprise value unless the
structure explicitly separates PropCo and OpCo economics (for example through market rent).

Where a PropCo/OpCo structure exists, reconcile:

- rent charged by PropCo to OpCo;
- property NOI and property valuation;
- OpCo EBITDA after rent;
- intercompany eliminations at consolidated level;
- exit assumptions for each component.

## Technical due diligence, lifecycle capex and marketability

For an acquisition, hold, refinance or redevelopment model, technical due diligence should connect to the
cash flow where physical findings are economically material. Translate the relevant condition into property
consequences such as:

- immediate repair or statutory-compliance capex;
- planned lifecycle replacement;
- downtime or restricted occupation;
- higher operating/maintenance cost;
- shortened economic life;
- impaired reletting or sale marketability;
- insurance/financing constraints; or
- refurbishment/redevelopment opportunity.

A technical report sitting outside the model does not neutralise an identified roof, façade, services,
structure, contamination or other material property issue. Conversely, do not create generic capex merely
because a technical-due-diligence standard exists; reflect actual asset condition and planned intervention.

Refurbishment/repositioning capex should not be modelled as an expense with no operating consequence when
the investment case assumes it restores competitiveness. Where supported, link it to the rent, occupancy,
letting cost, operating efficiency, useful life and/or exit yield that the intervention is expected to change.

## Sustainability, energy and climate value pathways — conditional

Sustainability or climate factors belong in a property model when they affect **market participant cash
flows, use, marketability or risk**. Do not apply a generic green premium, brown discount or ESG haircut.
Peer-reviewed evidence shows that energy/environmental characteristics can affect rents and prices in some
markets, but the magnitude and even statistical significance vary by asset, location, period and regulation.

Where material and supportable, propagate the factor through the specific pathway it affects, for example:

- retrofit/compliance capex and timing;
- energy, water or other operating cost and recoverability;
- achievable rent, incentives, occupancy and tenant demand;
- functional/regulatory obsolescence or legal ability to let/use the building;
- downtime during works;
- insurance availability/cost and physical-hazard exposure;
- debt availability/pricing where lenders differentiate the asset; and
- exit liquidity, buyer pool, economic life and yield/value.

Physical climate hazards such as flood, heat, fire, storm or water stress should likewise be modelled through
their evidenced property consequences rather than an arbitrary valuation haircut. Avoid double counting a
risk once in cash-flow impacts and again through an unsupported yield/discount-rate premium.

## Property finance and gearing — domain interface

Property commonly supports debt because the asset itself can provide collateral and recurring
income. Gearing changes the distribution of returns between debt and equity. The property-specific
principle is:

- when the property's economic return exceeds the cost of borrowing, leverage can increase the
  return on equity;
- when borrowing cost rises above property return or property income falls, leverage amplifies
  losses and can eliminate the equity return;
- the higher the gearing, the more sensitive equity value becomes to rent, vacancy, cap rate,
  interest rate and exit value.

Do not treat leverage-generated equity IRR as evidence that the underlying property is economically
strong. Review unlevered property economics separately from leveraged equity returns.

For development property, timing is particularly important because delays can simultaneously:

- defer sales/rent receipts;
- increase interest and holding costs;
- increase construction escalation;
- extend exposure to market movements.

### Financeability and capital-source fit for property development

A positive development NPV or margin does not by itself make a scheme financeable. Where a model is
being used to support funding, connect the finance case to the property facts on which capital providers
actually rely. Depending on the project and funding source, these can include:

- secure land ownership, acquisition rights or a documented landowner/JV arrangement;
- planning status, site clearance and other approvals needed before construction or drawdown;
- a feasibility case that reconciles the development scope, programme, cost and expected revenue;
- physical site evidence where material, including geotechnical, topographical, contamination or
  infrastructure constraints;
- pre-sales, pre-lets, prospective tenants or other evidence of demand where these are part of the
  funding case;
- projected sales/rent/NOI that reconcile to the same product mix, phasing and market assumptions as
  the development model; and
- an execution team and track record commensurate with the development scale where the financier is
  explicitly relying on sponsor/developer capability.

Do not turn this into a universal lender-document checklist. Different lenders, equity investors,
jurisdictions and project stages require different evidence. The property-domain question is whether the
model assumes funding is available **before the property conditions that support that funding are actually
satisfied**.

Capital sources also need to fit the stage and cash-flow profile of the property. Early land acquisition,
planning and pre-development expenditure may be funded differently from construction; a completed and
stabilised income-producing asset may support a refinancing structure that was unavailable during the
higher-risk development phase. Do not assume every finance instrument is interchangeable across these
stages.

### Funding tenor and property cash-flow duration

Real estate development can have a long period between land commitment and final settlements or
stabilised rent. A short-dated facility can therefore create refinancing/default risk even where the project
is profitable on an unlevered basis. Where the funding horizon is material, compare maturity and repayment
requirements with:

- acquisition and planning timing;
- construction duration and contingency;
- pre-sale/settlement timing or lease-up/stabilisation;
- practical completion versus final cash receipt; and
- realistic refinancing or extension availability if the original facility does not span the full cycle.

Do not assume that a loan can simply be rolled over at maturity. Conversely, do not require long-tenor debt
where the actual property strategy is a short, fully covered development-and-sale cycle.

### Pre-development funding and finance conditions

Senior development finance may not be available from the day a site is first identified. Land deposits,
planning, design, surveys, professional fees and other pre-development cash can arise before the project
has the approvals, pre-commitments or technical definition required by a construction lender.

Where the model assumes debt or other external capital during this period, identify the conditions that make
those funds available. A project can be economically attractive yet face a **pre-development funding gap**
if the sponsor must fund land and planning risk before the main facility becomes effective.

Also align financing approval/financial close with property commitments. The model should not assume a
facility is drawable after the developer has already become unconditionally liable for land settlement or
construction payments unless bridge/equity funding covers that timing.

### Pre-sales, pre-lets and off-taker financeability — conditional

Pre-sales and pre-lets can do two economically different things:

1. provide evidence of market demand and reduce perceived completion/absorption risk; and
2. in some structures, provide deposits or advance cash that can contribute to project funding.

Do not confuse these effects. A signed pre-sale or pre-let may improve lender confidence while providing
little or no immediately usable cash. Where purchaser/off-taker money is part of sources and uses, reflect
whether it is refundable, held in escrow/trust, released only after conditions are met, credited against the
final purchase price, or exposed to cancellation/default.

Where a lender requires a minimum level of pre-sales/pre-lets, compare the qualifying commitments with the
actual contractual definition used by the lender. Expressions of interest, unsigned reservations and weakly
committed buyers/tenants should not silently be treated as firm qualifying demand.

### Landowner joint ventures and deferred land consideration — conditional

A developer can reduce initial cash outlay by contributing development expertise/capital while a landowner
contributes the site. Economically, the land is **not free**. The model should identify how the landowner is
compensated, for example through:

- a fixed deferred land price;
- a share of units or sale proceeds;
- a preferred return or priority distribution;
- a percentage of project profit; or
- an equity/JV interest in the development vehicle.

The land contribution and the landowner's return should reconcile to the JV/transaction terms and should not
be counted again as both a land cost and a separate profit share unless the agreement genuinely requires both.

### Construction drawdown linked to property progress — conditional

Development lenders may release construction debt against certified cost/progress rather than simply making
the full facility available on day one. Where the financing terms use such a mechanism, connect drawdowns
to the property construction programme and applicable conditions, which may include quantity-surveyor,
engineer or lender-monitor certification, equity-first requirements, cost-to-complete tests or evidence that
prior draws were applied to the project.

Do not impose a particular certification process on every project. The domain test is whether debt availability
in the model is consistent with the actual property-development conditions required for drawdown.

### Local capital-market depth and funding availability

The availability of bank debt, mezzanine capital, REIT/equity capital, bonds, private investors, public-sector
support and other funding channels varies materially by jurisdiction, market maturity, asset class, sponsor
quality and transaction size. Country rankings or survey usage rates from one study are not portable market
benchmarks.

Where a model depends on a financing source that is unusual or thin in the project's actual market, require
transaction-specific evidence rather than assuming that because the instrument exists somewhere it is
available on the required amount, timing and terms for this property.


### Property-market, credit and development-cycle interaction

Real estate does not usually adjust instantaneously to changes in demand. Occupier demand can improve while
new supply remains constrained; rents may rise, vacancy may fall and yields/cap rates may compress before a
meaningful construction response is delivered. Development then arrives with a lag. If the broader economy or
credit environment weakens before that new space completes, the project can enter the market just as demand
softens, creating a combination of higher vacancy, weaker rents and outward yield movement.

Where a model spans a material development or holding period, do not extrapolate a single point in the property
cycle as though market conditions were static. Connect, where material:

- occupier demand and vacancy;
- market rent and leasing incentives;
- yield/cap-rate movement and capital value;
- development pipeline / competing supply and completion timing;
- availability and pricing of property credit; and
- the lag between a development decision, construction and delivery of new space.

A strong current market can therefore increase rather than eliminate future risk if it encourages simultaneous
construction, aggressive leverage or exit-value assumptions. Conversely, a weak current market should not be
assumed to persist indefinitely without a supported view on supply, demand and financing conditions.

### Cyclical property lending and refinancing conditions

Property lending standards and market liquidity can themselves vary with the property cycle. In stronger markets,
competition among capital providers may coincide with lower financing spreads, higher leverage and stronger
collateral values; in weaker markets, liquidity can retreat at the same time as property income and values weaken.

For a model that depends materially on refinance, extension or future debt availability, challenge any assumption
that today's LTV, margin, interest rate, amortisation, covenant or lender appetite will automatically be available at
the future refinance date. The downside case should consider the **joint property effect** of weaker NOI, softer
valuation and tighter credit rather than shocking each item independently only when their economic link matters.

Do not turn a historical crisis pattern into a forecast. The domain principle is that refinancing capacity is partly a
function of the future property market and future capital-market conditions, not merely the scheduled debt balance.

### Property securitisation / CMBS — conditional

Where property debt is financed, refinanced or valued through a mortgage-backed or securitised structure, trace the
economics back to the underlying income-producing properties and mortgages. Relevant property-specific risks can
include:

- tenant/occupier ability to support the rent that services the mortgage;
- vacancy, reletting downtime and property-level NOI deterioration;
- collateral value and LTV migration as market yields/cap rates change;
- concentration by asset, tenant, geography or property type;
- interest-only or balloon structures that leave material principal due at maturity;
- prepayment and, particularly for commercial property, **extension/refinancing risk** where expected take-out debt
  is unavailable; and
- senior/subordinated tranche priority where losses or cash flows are allocated differently across investors.

Do not infer that securitisation removes the underlying property risk. It redistributes and packages that risk. Where
a model represents only the property borrower, apply only the terms that actually affect that borrower; do not add
tranche mechanics that sit entirely outside the project vehicle.

### Listed property / REIT value versus direct-property NAV — conditional

A listed property vehicle or REIT can trade at a premium or discount to the appraised/net asset value of its underlying
properties. Public-market price and direct-property NAV are therefore related but not interchangeable valuation bases.
Where a model uses both, keep clear whether value is being derived from:

- direct property-level cash flows and market valuation;
- net asset value after vehicle-level debt and other claims; or
- the public-market price of the listed vehicle.

Do not force a listed market price to equal appraised NAV merely because they reference the same assets, and do not
count a premium/discount to NAV as an additional asset-level cash flow. Any premium/discount used in a decision
case should be treated as a market observation or scenario, not as proof that the underlying property valuation is wrong.

### Cross-border property investment and currency exposure — conditional

International property investment combines local property economics with investor-currency exposure and local
institutional conditions. Diversification benefits are not automatic: they can be reduced or reversed by exchange-rate
volatility, correlated economic cycles, taxes, transaction costs, market opacity, legal/property-right differences and
capital controls or other investment constraints.

Where the investment return is reported in a currency different from the property's operating currency, distinguish:

1. local-currency property income and capital return;
2. financing in local or foreign currency;
3. hedging, if any; and
4. translation/repatriation into the investor's reporting currency.

Do not claim international diversification solely because the assets are in different countries, and do not assume a
local-currency return is equivalent to the investor's home-currency return.

### Refinancing / recapitalisation as a property monetisation path

A property investment may monetise value through sale, cash-out refinance/recapitalisation or a
partial interest transaction. Where cash-out refinancing is modelled, test whether the property has
reached the income/value condition used to size the new debt, whether refinance fees and repayment
of the old facility are included, and whether extracted cash is treated as financing proceeds rather
than operating profit.

Do not count both refinance proceeds and the same underlying value again at exit without carrying
the replacement debt through the remaining holding period. Sale and refinance can both occur in a
strategy, but only if the model shows the actual sequence rather than using both as independent
full-value exits.

### Sponsor / investor JV and promote economics

Where the property is owned through a sponsor-investor partnership or JV, the economic sequence
can include contributed equity, return of capital, preferred return/hurdles, changing tier splits and a
sponsor promote. The exact percentages and hurdle rates are transaction terms, not market defaults.

Reconcile operating, refinancing and sale distributions to the cash actually available from the
property and to the governing JV terms. Do not allow a promote/waterfall to distribute cash that is
not distributable at the property level or that must first satisfy a senior property obligation.

### Sale and leaseback — conditional

A sale-and-leaseback releases capital while preserving occupation, but it also creates or retains a
future rent obligation. The value of the sold interest can differ materially from vacant-possession or
unencumbered value because the new lease changes the cash flows and risk received by the buyer.
Where a sale-and-leaseback is modelled, reflect the actual leaseback rent, term, reviews/escalation,
breaks, repair/insurance obligations, tenant covenant and transaction costs.

Do not present the sale proceeds as a free financing benefit while ignoring the lease liability/economic
occupancy cost created by the transaction. Separate property-sale proceeds from the future leaseback
cash flows so the transaction can be compared with alternative financing or continued ownership on
a consistent basis.

### Lease restructuring / surrender and renewal — conditional

Where an existing lease is surrendered, renewed or materially varied, compare the landlord and
tenant economic positions **before and after** the restructuring. Changes in rent, term, incentives,
repair obligations or capital contributions can transfer value between the parties. If one party gains
and the other loses, a premium or other consideration may be needed to make the transaction
commercially acceptable.

### Lending/security valuation and realisability

A property used as loan security should be valued on the explicitly stated basis relevant to the
lending decision. Do not treat loan-to-value or a lender haircut as a substitute for property value.
Where a lender requests a constrained-sale or special assumption, make the assumed marketing
period and conditions explicit rather than presenting a generic "forced sale value" as a stable fact.

Security analysis should also recognise realisability. A specialised property with limited buyer
depth, high conversion cost or weak market evidence can be poor collateral even if replacement
cost is high. Today's valuation is not a guarantee of proceeds on a future default date; market
conditions, property condition and sale circumstances may then be different.

## Property-specific taxes and statutory charges

Property is often exposed to transaction, ownership, development and consumption taxes that vary
materially by jurisdiction. Relevant examples may include GST/VAT, transfer/stamp duty, land tax,
municipal rates, development contributions and capital-gains/income tax consequences.

Do not import historical tax percentages or rules from this source. The property-domain discipline is
to identify which taxes attach to acquisition, construction, leasing, sale and holding, and ensure the
model uses one coherent tax basis for the jurisdiction and transaction structure.

## Property-specific risk lenses

A single IRR/NPV does not describe the full property risk. Depending on asset and purpose, challenge
the model through property-specific lenses such as:

- **entry price / cost basis** — a good asset can still be a poor investment if acquired too
  expensively;
- **environmental / site condition** — contamination, remediation, geotechnical or other physical
  site issues can change cost, timing, permitted use and value;
- **planning / legislative risk** — approvals, zoning/use restrictions and policy can change the
  development envelope;
- **market / vacancy risk** — distinguish headline physical vacancy from actual lease-level economic
  loss;
- **tenant / resident risk** — tenant financial strength, renewal and concentration can drive
  property cash flow;
- **physical / appeal risk** — ageing roof/HVAC/building systems, deferred maintenance,
  specification and amenity obsolescence can require lifecycle/repositioning capex;
- **exit price / terminal yield risk** — future cap rate is difficult to forecast and can dominate
  value;
- **holding-period risk** — the economics can change materially if sale, refinance or continued hold
  occurs at a different date.

Do not make a Phase I/Phase II environmental report or any particular engineering report mandatory
for every property model. If a site/property condition is decision-critical and the spreadsheet cannot
establish it, request the relevant evidence and model the cost, delay, use restriction or limitation
rather than assuming it away.

Probabilistic analysis can be useful where rent, vacancy, costs, exit yield, timing or other property
drivers have supportable ranges/distributions. Do not turn a deterministic sensitivity into a
probability statement, and do not impose a normal distribution simply for convenience. Staiger's
P(Gain) concept is a useful optional lens on the probability of returning invested capital, not a
universal property standard.

## Common property-model failure patterns

1. **Residual land value treated as an independent input:** land value is both hard-coded and also
   derived residually from GDV less costs, creating circular or inconsistent economics.
2. **GDV not tied to physical saleable inventory:** headline GDV is entered independently of units,
   area, product mix and sale prices.
3. **Completed value and stabilised value conflated:** the model capitalises stabilised income at
   practical completion even though lease-up/operating ramp-up takes time.
4. **Comparable evidence not genuinely comparable:** $/sqm or $/unit evidence ignores location,
   tenure, specification, condition, timing or transaction circumstances.
5. **Replacement cost treated as market value:** development or book cost is presented as value
   without depreciation, obsolescence or market support.
6. **Passing rent capitalised as perpetual market rent:** below/above-market contract rent is used
   forever with no lease expiry/reversion logic.
7. **Market rent recognised before legally available:** model uplifts rent immediately despite rent
   review/expiry occurring years later.
8. **Gross income capitalised at a net yield:** outgoings, vacancy or landlord costs are omitted while
   a net-income capitalisation rate is applied.
9. **Leasehold valued like freehold:** finite term, head rent, restrictions or expiry are ignored.
10. **Planning uplift treated as certain:** unapproved density/change of use is valued as though final
    permission already exists.
11. **Residual valuation false precision:** large uncertain GDV and cost inputs produce a highly
    precise residual land value with no sensitivity to core drivers.
12. **Development costs omit realisation costs:** professional, finance, marketing/leasing/sales,
    statutory or holding costs required to realise GDV are omitted.
13. **Practical completion equals settlement:** all sale cash is received at completion without
    contract/settlement lag.
14. **Land timing ignores deposits/settlement:** acquisition cash flows do not follow actual property
    transaction timing.
15. **Operating property value double counted with OpCo value:** property capitalisation is added to
    business value even though the operating earnings already include the benefit of rent-free use of
    the property.
16. **Yield chosen independently of property risk:** cap rate/yield is copied from a benchmark without
    adjustment for lease, location, quality, vacancy, capex or growth differences.
17. **DCF terminal value inconsistent with stabilised property economics:** terminal NOI and cap rate
    imply a value that does not reconcile to the model's stated property valuation assumptions.
18. **Leverage hides weak property economics:** high equity IRR is driven by debt while unlevered
    property return is below the required return or highly fragile.
19. **Redevelopment option ignores current-use opportunity cost:** redevelopment analysis omits the
    value/income sacrificed by closing, demolishing or selling the existing use.
20. **Specialist trading property capitalises operator skill:** exceptional business performance is
    treated entirely as property rental capacity without separating operator remuneration and
    business risk.
21. **Terminal value uses levered or wrong-period income:** debt service or a non-representative
    current period is capitalised instead of supportable forward property NOI.
22. **Gross property sale value and equity proceeds are conflated:** disposal costs and debt payoff
    are mixed into the cap-rate value rather than bridged separately.
23. **Static physical vacancy is treated as economic vacancy:** a headline vacancy percentage is
    applied without reflecting lease-level downtime, concessions, lost recoveries and reletting costs.
24. **Vacancy and seasonality are double counted:** a monthly occupancy/seasonality profile already
    reduces revenue and a second generic vacancy haircut captures the same loss.
25. **Rent-roll summary is detached from tenant/unit economics:** detailed lease data exist but
    headline property revenue follows an independent assumption.
26. **Rollover economics omit owner cash-flow disrupters:** rent is reset on expiry but downtime,
    tenant improvements, leasing commissions or lost recoveries remain unchanged.
27. **Tenant concentration is hidden by tenant count:** several tenants share one expiry, sector,
    guarantor or demand driver but are treated as fully diversified.
28. **CAM/service-charge recoveries are treated as free revenue:** recoveries do not reconcile to
    recoverable expenses, occupancy, area allocation or lease terms.
29. **Hotel revenue is detached from physical room capacity:** occupancy, available rooms and ADR do
    not reconcile to room revenue.
30. **Hotel stabilisation is compressed into one generic escalation:** occupancy and ADR have
    materially different ramp-up or seasonal behaviour but the model forces one common growth path.
31. **Cash-out refinance is treated as operating profit:** financing proceeds inflate property
    operating performance rather than being shown as a financing/monetisation event.
32. **Refinance value is counted twice:** cash is extracted at recapitalisation and the same underlying
    value is recovered again at exit without carrying the replacement debt.
33. **Sale and refinance are both modelled as independent full exits:** the strategy uses two
    mutually inconsistent monetisation paths in the same period.
34. **JV waterfall terms are imported from convention rather than the transaction:** preferred return,
    hurdle or promote percentages are assumed without support.
35. **JV distributions exceed property distributable cash:** the promote/waterfall allocates cash
    before a senior property obligation or without reconciling to property-level cash available.
36. **Environmental/site-condition risk is assumed away:** a decision-critical contamination,
    remediation or site constraint has no evidence, cost, delay or limitation.
37. **Physical/appeal obsolescence is omitted:** prime rent/occupancy/yield assumptions persist while
    lifecycle replacement or repositioning capex is absent.
38. **Exit yield and holding period are treated as certain:** value is dominated by a distant terminal
    cap rate or fixed monetisation date with no property-specific downside challenge.
39. **Multiple-sign-change return ambiguity is ignored:** acquisition, later capital calls/refinance
    and exit create a cash-flow pattern for which one IRR may be non-unique or misleading.
40. **Probabilistic output is built from unsupported distributions:** sensitivity ranges or convenient
    normal distributions are presented as confidence/probability without property evidence.
41. **Market value and investor-specific worth are mixed:** buyer-specific financing, tax, synergy or
    strategic benefit is embedded in a value presented as market value without separation.
42. **Area-basis mismatch:** cost, rent, sale price or density rates are multiplied by an inconsistent
    gross/net/lettable/saleable area basis.
43. **Comparable evidence is mechanically averaged:** weak/stale/non-arm's-length evidence receives
    the same weight as recent, genuinely comparable market evidence.
44. **Retail zoning basis mismatch:** a zoned/ITZA comparable is applied to whole retail floor area or
    vice versa without a market-supported conversion.
45. **Implicit and explicit growth are double counted:** explicit rent/capital growth is added while a
    yield basis that already embeds the same growth expectation is applied without reconciliation.
46. **Income timing and yield convention are inconsistent:** quarterly/monthly rent in advance is
    capitalised using an incompatible annual/arrears or nominal/effective yield basis.
47. **Headline rent is treated as effective rent:** rent-free periods, stepped rents, cash/fit-out
    contributions or other incentives are ignored in comparable-rent analysis.
48. **Lease-term comparables are not normalised:** materially different review frequency, breaks or
    lease length are compared directly with no economic adjustment or explanation.
49. **Over-rent is capitalised as permanent value:** above-market passing rent is treated as perpetual
    without expiry/review/default/re-letting economics; or a tenant onerous-lease liability is ignored.
50. **Sale-and-leaseback proceeds are treated as free value:** capital released is recognised without
    the resulting leaseback obligation or the changed value of the sold interest.
51. **Lease restructure transfers value invisibly:** surrender/renewal or variation changes one party's
    economics without modelling the premium/compensation or before/after positions.
52. **Synergistic value is double counted or universalised:** a special purchaser's combination value
    is included in standalone value and again as a premium, or treated as available to all market buyers.
53. **Security value is confused with lending metric:** LTV, lender haircut or an assumed forced-sale
    percentage is presented as the property's intrinsic market value.
54. **Regulatory/energy obsolescence is ignored:** terminal rent/value assumes continued prime
    marketability while required compliance/repositioning capex or impaired lettability is omitted.
55. **Residual site value lacks a market cross-check:** a highly sensitive residual is accepted without
    comparison to available site/development-opportunity evidence or another supportable benchmark.
56. **Turnover rent uses the wrong contractual base:** percentage rent is applied to sales/receipts that
    are excluded by the lease, or base/minimum rent and thresholds are omitted.
57. **Funding source is inconsistent with development stage:** construction-style senior debt is assumed
    during land/planning risk before the conditions supporting that facility are satisfied, or a stabilised-asset
    refinancing source is treated as available during development with no bridge.
58. **Finance tenor expires before the property cash cycle:** debt matures before construction, settlement or
    stabilisation is realistically achieved, with an unsupported rollover/refinancing assumption.
59. **Funding case ignores a pre-development gap:** land deposits, planning, design or site studies arise
    before the main facility is drawable but no sponsor/bridge/equity source covers them.
60. **Pre-sales/pre-lets are treated as unrestricted cash:** commitments are counted as project funding even
    though deposits are refundable, escrowed, conditional or not yet legally available.
61. **Soft demand is treated as qualifying lender pre-commitment:** expressions of interest or non-binding
    reservations are counted toward a pre-sale/pre-let condition that requires enforceable commitments.
62. **Landowner JV creates 'free land':** the model reduces acquisition cash outlay but omits the landowner's
    deferred price, profit share, units, preferred return or equity interest.
63. **Construction debt is drawn ahead of eligible property progress:** the model uses the full facility even
    though actual terms require certified works, equity contribution or other property drawdown conditions.
64. **Financeability package is detached from the modelled property:** title, permits, feasibility, pre-sales,
    projected revenue or site evidence support one scheme while the spreadsheet assumes a materially
    different development scope, phasing, cost or product mix.
65. **Locally unavailable finance is treated as certain:** the project relies on a funding instrument or capital
    pool with no credible availability for the jurisdiction, asset class, transaction size or sponsor.
66. **Draft standards are treated as current rules:** a consultation/exposure draft or archived measurement/
    valuation edition is imposed as though operative for the valuation date and jurisdiction.
67. **Point-estimate precision exceeds property evidence:** a thin/volatile market or highly sensitive residual
    is presented to false precision with no recognition of the economically credible range.
68. **Land-area definitions are conflated:** title/site/net-development area are treated as interchangeable,
    distorting density, cost allocation, land rates or GDV.
69. **Terminal value dominates without a property bridge:** exit value uses current or unsupported forward NOI,
    prime yield and condition assumptions that do not reconcile to the asset at the exit date.
70. **Location growth is mistaken for asset growth:** an ageing/obsolete property receives full market rent growth
    with no asset-specific depreciation or refurbishment effect.
71. **Cost-to-complete still equals original budget less spend:** known changes, commitments, claims, defects or
    consumed contingency do not update the remaining development requirement.
72. **Entitlement and construction are collapsed into one irreversible decision:** planning-stage optionality or the
    real constraints on waiting are ignored.
73. **Work-in-progress is valued as completed GDV less unspent budget:** remaining execution, contractual,
    warranty, claim and remobilisation risks are not reflected.
74. **Residual output is ambiguous:** a land residual, developer profit and/or target return are mixed or double
    counted in the same appraisal.
75. **Service-charge recovery is recognised on the wrong timing/basis:** ultimate recoverability is assumed to
    remove landlord cash-funding gaps, lease exclusions or vacancy leakage.
76. **Technical findings do not reach the cash flow:** material repair, lifecycle or condition issues exist but rent,
    capex, downtime, economic life and exit remain unchanged.
77. **Refurbishment capex has no property consequence:** repositioning spend is incurred but the assumed rent,
    occupancy, useful life or exit economics do not change, or those benefits are assumed without the spend.
78. **Generic ESG/climate haircut substitutes for property economics:** sustainability or physical-risk effects are
    applied as an unexplained percentage instead of through evidenced capex, income, marketability, insurance,
    financing or exit pathways.

## Property-specific contradiction patterns

Use these as directional property-professional challenges rather than automatic failures:

- **Area / value contradiction:** rentable or saleable area changes materially while rent/GDV is
  unchanged despite unchanged unit rates, or cost changes with the wrong area denominator.
- **Incentive / rent contradiction:** headline rent rises while rent-free/cash incentives also increase,
  yet effective rent is reported as unchanged or higher without support.
- **Over-rent / covenant contradiction:** passing rent is materially above market and tenant credit
  weakens, but value/risk assumptions improve or remain unchanged.
- **Growth / yield contradiction:** explicit rental growth is increased while the model also tightens a
  yield calibrated on an implicit-growth basis, with no market rationale.
- **Leaseback / financing contradiction:** sale-and-leaseback proceeds improve equity returns while
  future occupancy cost remains as though the property were still owned free of rent.
- **Security / liquidity contradiction:** specialised/illiquid property is assigned stronger collateral
  realisability solely because replacement cost rises.
- **Obsolescence / exit contradiction:** regulatory or physical requirements increase expected capex
  but terminal rent, occupancy and yield remain at prime-asset assumptions.
- **Funding / approval contradiction:** development debt is shown as available before the title, planning,
  pre-sale/pre-let or other stated conditions required for that facility are achieved.
- **Tenor / completion contradiction:** facility maturity moves earlier than completion/settlement while the
  project return is unchanged because refinancing is assumed automatically.
- **Pre-sale / cash contradiction:** pre-sales increase materially but usable project cash does not follow the
  contractual deposit/release terms, or restricted deposits are treated as freely drawable.
- **Landowner-JV contradiction:** cash land cost falls to zero but no compensating landowner distribution,
  unit allocation or equity interest appears elsewhere.
- **Drawdown / progress contradiction:** construction debt rises materially while certified/eligible project
  spend or physical progress does not.

- **Age / rent contradiction:** the asset ages materially while receiving full market/location growth and
  retaining prime relative rent without refurbishment or evidence that obsolescence is immaterial.
- **Refurbishment / exit contradiction:** substantial repositioning capex is removed or delayed but exit rent,
  occupancy and yield remain at the post-refurbishment case.
- **WIP / completion contradiction:** construction is materially incomplete or subject to claims, yet valuation
  jumps directly to full completed-asset value with no remaining time/cost/risk bridge.
- **Sustainability / cash-flow contradiction:** a regulation or hazard is stated to impair lettability/insurability
  but rent, vacancy, capex and exit assumptions remain unchanged; or a green premium is added without any
  market/cash-flow pathway.

## Property dependency chains

### Build-to-sell development

```
Site / tenure / planning rights
  → permissible development envelope
  → unit / area / product mix
  → achievable selling prices
  → GDV / gross realisation

Design + programme + site conditions
  → construction / infrastructure / professional costs
  → timing of spend
  → finance / holding cost
  → total development cost

Land/title + approvals + feasibility + market commitments
  → financeability conditions
  → eligible funding source / drawdown timing
  → funded construction and settlement cycle

Pre-sales / pre-lets / off-taker commitments
  → demand evidence + any contractually releasable deposits
  → lender confidence / project liquidity where applicable

GDV - total development cost
  → development profit
  → development margin / NPV / IRR

GDV - all development costs - required developer return
  → residual land/site value
```

### Income-producing property

```
Physical asset + location + legal interest + permitted use
  → occupier demand / market rent
  → lease terms + passing rent + review/expiry
  → gross property income
  → vacancy / incentives / landlord outgoings
  → NOI / net property cash flow
  → yield / cap rate or DCF
  → property value
```

### Develop-and-operate

```
Development programme + cost + completion
  → operational opening
  → occupancy / utilisation / trading ramp-up
  → operating earnings

Property market rent / NOI
  → property value (PropCo lens)

Operating earnings after economic property occupancy cost
  → operating-business value (OpCo lens)

PropCo value + OpCo value - duplicated economics
  → combined enterprise/equity value
```

## Domain-specific graded tests

These tests add property-professional challenges to the existing core audit framework. They do not
replace generic formula, evidence, reporting or governance procedures.

### T2-S10-201 — test: property_valuation_purpose_and_interest
Identify the exact property interest, valuation date and valuation purpose represented by the model.
Confirm the model does not mix transaction price, market value, investor-specific worth/investment
value, replacement cost, residual site value, rental value or another basis as though they were
interchangeable.

### T2-S10-202 — test: property_comparable_evidence_normalisation
Where comparable transactions or lettings support value/rent, test whether evidence is adjusted for
material differences in location, size, specification, condition, tenure, lease terms, planning/use and
transaction date. A raw average of non-comparable $/sqm or $/unit evidence is insufficient.

### T2-S10-203 — test: property_market_evidence_recency
Compare the valuation date with the dates of material comparable evidence. Where market,
financing, planning or supply/demand conditions changed materially, require an explicit adjustment
or explanation before historical evidence is relied upon.

### T2-S10-204 — test: property_residual_value_complete_cost_basis
For a residual/development valuation, reconcile completed value less all costs required to create and
realise the scheme, including relevant acquisition, construction, professional, statutory, finance,
holding and sales/leasing costs plus required developer return.

### T2-S10-205 — test: property_residual_sensitivity
Test residual land/site value against material movements in selling price/rent, construction cost,
programme and finance cost. Flag false precision where small plausible changes materially alter or
reverse the residual value.

### T2-S10-206 — test: property_planning_entitlement_certainty
Identify which modelled development rights are existing/approved versus conditional, pending or
aspirational. Confirm unapproved higher-use economics are not presented with approved-scheme
certainty.

### T2-S10-207 — test: property_passing_market_rent_reversion
Where passing rent differs from market rent, verify that current contracted income, review/expiry,
reletting and market-rent reversion are reflected at the correct dates rather than capitalising one rent
in perpetuity.

### T2-S10-208 — test: property_net_income_capitalisation_basis
Where value is derived from a yield/cap rate, confirm the income measure and yield basis match —
for example, net property income with a net yield. Check vacancy, incentives and landlord-borne
outgoings are treated consistently with the market evidence.

### T2-S10-209 — test: property_leasehold_term_and_headrent
For leasehold or sublease interests, reconcile remaining term, head rent, rent reviews, subletting
rights and expiry/reversion. Reject treatment economically equivalent to freehold where the finite
interest materially affects value.

### T2-S10-210 — test: property_cost_value_distinction
Where replacement or development cost is used as a valuation cross-check, verify depreciation,
obsolescence and market utility are considered. Cost alone is not evidence of market value.

### T2-S10-211 — test: property_gdv_inventory_reconciliation
For build-to-sell models, reconcile GDV to saleable units/areas, product mix and selling prices. A
headline GDV not traceable to the physical sales inventory is a property-specific failure pattern.

### T2-S10-212 — test: property_completion_stabilisation_exit_dates
Distinguish practical completion, sale/lease commencement, settlement, stabilisation and exit.
Confirm rents, sale proceeds and valuation uplift occur at the economic dates supported by the
project structure.

### T2-S10-213 — test: property_dcf_terminal_capitalisation_reconciliation
Where a property DCF uses a terminal value, reconcile terminal NOI, vacancy/outgoings and terminal
cap rate to the model's stabilised property assumptions and any direct-capitalisation cross-check.

### T2-S10-214 — test: property_unlevered_vs_equity_return
Where leverage is used, compare unlevered property/development economics with equity returns.
Identify cases where debt alone creates an attractive equity IRR while the underlying property return
is weak or highly sensitive.

### T2-S10-215 — test: property_redevelopment_opportunity_cost
For redevelopment or change-of-use cases, include the economically relevant value/income of the
existing asset that is surrendered, plus closure/demolition/relocation costs where applicable. Do not
compare a redevelopment NPV against zero if the existing property has value.

### T2-S10-216 — test: property_specialist_trading_maintainable_earnings
For hotels, leisure, hospitality or other specialist trading property using a profits/trading-potential
approach, distinguish maintainable property earning capacity from exceptional operator skill,
remuneration, business capital and operating risk.

### T2-S10-217 — test: property_propco_opco_double_count
For develop-and-operate/PropCo-OpCo structures, reconcile market rent, property NOI and OpCo
EBITDA. Confirm the same occupancy economics are not capitalised once in property value and again
inside business value.

### T2-S10-218 — test: property_highest_use_development_option
Where multiple development alternatives exist, test that the selected option is economically and
legally supportable after considering planning, density, buildable area, programme, cost, absorption,
financing and achievable value. Do not assume maximum floor area equals maximum land value.


### T2-S10-219 — test: property_terminal_forward_noi_basis
For an income-producing exit valuation, confirm the capitalised income represents supportable
forward/next-period property NOI and excludes debt service. Challenge terminal values built from an
arbitrary single month or a non-representative period.

### T2-S10-220 — test: property_sale_value_to_equity_proceeds_bridge
Reconcile gross property sale value to net sale proceeds after disposal costs, then separately to
equity proceeds after repayment of debt/other financing. Confirm financing settlement is not embedded
inside the underlying property valuation.

### T2-S10-221 — test: property_rent_roll_to_revenue_reconciliation
For multi-unit leased property, reconcile aggregate rental revenue to tenant/unit lease economics,
including area, passing rent, reviews/escalation, abatements and occupancy status where material.

### T2-S10-222 — test: property_rollover_economic_vacancy
For material lease expiries, test the owner economics of renewal/reletting: downtime, rent reset,
lost recoveries, tenant improvements/fit-out and leasing commissions. Do not substitute a static
physical-vacancy percentage for the actual economic vacancy when lease-level evidence exists.

### T2-S10-223 — test: property_vacancy_seasonality_double_count
Where monthly seasonality or occupancy profiles are used, determine whether a separate vacancy
deduction represents a distinct risk or double counts the same expected lost revenue.

### T2-S10-224 — test: property_recoveries_cam_basis
For CAM/service-charge/expense recoveries, reconcile the recovery base to recoverable property
expenses, occupied area/tenant allocation, caps/floors and vacancy leakage where applicable.

### T2-S10-225 — test: property_tenant_concentration_credit
Identify material tenant concentration by rent, area, lease expiry, sector or guarantor strength.
Where a major tenant drives value, assess the property cash-flow consequence of non-renewal/default
rather than relying only on a generic bad-debt percentage.

### T2-S10-226 — test: property_hotel_room_revenue_driver
For hotel/short-stay property, reconcile room revenue to available room nights, occupancy and ADR
at the relevant room-type/period granularity. Occupied room nights must not exceed available room
nights.

### T2-S10-227 — test: property_hotel_stabilisation_seasonality
Where hotel opening/ramp-up is material, test occupancy and ADR separately and preserve supported
monthly/seasonal patterns. Challenge a single escalation factor that masks distinct demand and rate
dynamics.

### T2-S10-228 — test: property_recapitalisation_cashout_refi
Where cash-out refinance/recapitalisation is assumed, confirm the property has achieved the
income/value condition used to size new debt, refinance costs and old-debt repayment are included,
and extracted cash is treated as financing proceeds rather than operating income.

### T2-S10-229 — test: property_exit_path_consistency
Where sale, refinance and partial-interest monetisation alternatives exist, confirm the model applies
the actual sequence for the selected strategy and does not count the same property value through
multiple independent full exits.

### T2-S10-230 — test: property_jv_waterfall_economic_terms
For sponsor/investor JVs, reconcile contributed capital, preferred return/hurdles, return of capital,
tier splits and promote to the governing transaction terms. Do not import example market percentages
as defaults.

### T2-S10-231 — test: property_jv_distributable_cash_reconciliation
Reconcile operating, refinance and sale distributions through the JV waterfall to property-level cash
actually available after senior property obligations required by the transaction.

### T2-S10-232 — test: property_environmental_site_risk
Where contamination, remediation or another site condition can affect the investment case, confirm
the model reflects the supported cost/timing/use impact or clearly identifies the evidence needed.
Do not assume a clean site solely because no allowance appears in the spreadsheet.

### T2-S10-233 — test: property_physical_appeal_lifecycle_capex
Test whether assumed rents, occupancy and exit quality are consistent with building age, deferred
maintenance, major systems and refurbishment/amenity requirements. Challenge prime economics
that require reinvestment the model never funds.

### T2-S10-234 — test: property_entry_price_margin_of_safety
Assess whether acquisition/cost basis is separated from operating property quality. A strong asset
does not make an overpaid entry price economically safe; compare the return/value response to
supportable entry-price variation.

### T2-S10-235 — test: property_exit_cap_rate_risk
Where terminal value is material, challenge the exit cap rate/yield against property quality, income
durability, remaining lease term, capex burden and market context, and test downside rather than
assuming the entry yield persists.

### T2-S10-236 — test: property_holding_period_risk
Where the investment can be sold, refinanced or retained at different dates, assess whether the
chosen holding period materially changes capex, lease rollover, income maturity, debt and exit value.
Do not treat a hard-coded hold as economically neutral.

### T2-S10-237 — test: property_multiple_sign_change_return_metric
Inspect property/equity cash flows for multiple sign changes caused by later capital calls,
refurbishment, recapitalisation or other events. Where present, do not rely on a single IRR without
a date-based NPV/value anchor and an explanation of any alternative return measure.

### T2-S10-238 — test: property_probabilistic_driver_support
Where Monte Carlo/probabilistic analysis is used, confirm the selected property drivers, ranges,
distributions and material dependencies are supported by evidence. Sensitivity bounds are not
probabilities.

### T2-S10-239 — test: property_return_of_capital_probability
Where a P(Gain) or similar probability-of-return-of-capital metric is used, verify the loss threshold,
cash-flow definition and probability assumptions align with the actual investor/property economics.
Treat the metric as supplementary, not a substitute for downside cash-flow analysis.

### T2-S10-240 — test: property_repositioning_optimisation_feasibility
Where unit mix, use, pricing, leverage, density or holding period is optimised, confirm the proposed
solution remains physically buildable, legally permissible, financeable and supportable by market
absorption/demand. A mathematical optimum outside real property constraints is not decision-useful.

### T2-S10-241 — test: property_measurement_basis_consistency
Map each material property area definition used by the model — for example gross/built, lettable,
saleable, planning/GFA and site area — and reconcile the conversion/efficiency ratios. Confirm each
cost, rent, sale-price and density rate is applied to the same measurement basis as its source evidence.

### T2-S10-242 — test: property_comparable_weighting_and_transaction_context
For material sales/rent comparables, test whether the model records enough context to distinguish
recent arm's-length evidence from stale, related-party, bundled, sale-and-leaseback or otherwise
less-direct evidence. Confirm weighting reflects relevance/reliability rather than a mechanical average.

### T2-S10-243 — test: property_retail_zoning_basis
Where retail evidence uses zoning/ITZA/frontage-depth analysis, confirm the subject property is
measured and valued on the same local market convention. Do not impose zoning where the relevant
market does not use it, and do not apply a zoned rate to undifferentiated whole floor area.

### T2-S10-244 — test: property_yield_basis_taxonomy_consistency
Identify whether each material yield is initial, reversionary, equivalent/implicit-growth,
equated/explicit-growth or another market convention. Confirm the income, growth and timing basis
used in the valuation is consistent with the way the yield was derived.

### T2-S10-245 — test: property_income_timing_yield_convention
Where rent-payment timing is material, compare actual monthly/quarterly/half-yearly/annual and
advance/arrears cash flows with the convention underlying the market yield/capitalisation factor.
Require a coherent conversion where the bases differ.

### T2-S10-246 — test: property_lease_incentive_effective_rent
For material lease incentives, reconcile headline rent to effective economic rent after rent-free
periods, stepped rents, cash/fit-out contributions, service-charge concessions and other inducements.
Use a supportable analysis/write-off period and compare like-with-like rent evidence.

### T2-S10-247 — test: property_abnormal_review_term_normalisation
Where the subject lease has materially different rent-review frequency, break rights or lease length
from comparable evidence, test whether the economic difference has been normalised or supported as
immaterial using current market evidence rather than a universal textbook adjustment.

### T2-S10-248 — test: property_overrented_reverse_premium
Where passing rent materially exceeds market rent, isolate the finite excess-rent cash flow and test
default/re-letting, review/break/expiry and tenant-liability economics. For onerous leaseholds, assess
whether assignment/surrender requires a reverse premium or other compensation.

### T2-S10-249 — test: property_lease_restructure_before_after_economics
For surrender, renewal or material lease variation, compare landlord and tenant economic positions
before and after the restructure. Reconcile any rent change, incentive, capital contribution or
premium needed to transfer value between the parties.

### T2-S10-250 — test: property_sale_leaseback_value_and_obligation
For sale-and-leaseback transactions, reconcile sale proceeds to the value of the interest actually sold
and model the resulting leaseback rent, term, reviews/breaks, covenant, obligations and transaction
costs. Confirm released capital is not treated as a free benefit with no future occupancy cost.

### T2-S10-251 — test: property_synergistic_value_separation
Where combination of parcels or legal interests creates incremental value, value the standalone
interests and the combination on a coherent basis, identify which party can realise the synergy and
confirm the increment is neither universalised to all buyers nor double counted.

### T2-S10-252 — test: property_lending_security_realisability_basis
For lending/security cases, identify the stated property valuation basis and any special sale
assumptions. Keep market value separate from LTV/haircuts, and challenge collateral recoverability
where buyer depth, specialisation, conversion cost or marketability limits realisation.

### T2-S10-253 — test: property_turnover_rent_contract_basis
Where rent is turnover/percentage based, reconcile base/minimum rent, applicable turnover measure,
percentage, thresholds, exclusions, caps/floors and reporting period to the lease economics. Do not
treat operator-specific exceptional sales as ordinary property rent without support.

### T2-S10-254 — test: property_regulatory_obsolescence_capex_value
Where energy, flood, accessibility, building-services, climate-resilience or other current regulatory/
market requirements affect use or marketability, reconcile required capex/timing with rent, vacancy,
economic life and exit value. Do not import a dated statutory threshold as a universal rule.

### T2-S10-255 — test: property_residual_market_crosscheck
Where a residual development/site value is material and credible market evidence exists, compare the
residual with comparable development-site transactions or another supportable market indicator.
Investigate material divergence rather than accepting the residual solely because its arithmetic closes.


### T2-S10-256 — test: property_finance_source_stage_and_tenor_fit
Where a property development relies on external capital, compare the assumed source, availability date,
maturity and repayment profile with the actual development stage and property cash cycle. Challenge
construction/stabilised-asset finance used before its conditions are supportable, and short-dated funding that
expires before realistic completion, settlement or stabilisation without evidenced bridge/refinancing capacity.

### T2-S10-257 — test: property_financeability_evidence_package
For a model used to support funding, reconcile the financed scheme with the property evidence on which the
capital provider relies, as applicable: land/title or acquisition rights, approvals/site clearance, feasibility,
site/geotechnical/topographical evidence, development cost/programme, market demand, pre-sales/pre-lets,
prospective tenants and projected sales/rent/NOI. Do not impose a universal document list; test consistency
with the actual transaction and lender/investor conditions.

### T2-S10-258 — test: property_predevelopment_funding_gap
Map land deposits/settlement, planning, design, surveys, professional fees and other pre-development cash
against the date the main development facility becomes effective/drawable. Confirm sponsor equity, bridge or
another credible source funds the period before senior construction finance is available.

### T2-S10-259 — test: property_presale_prelet_offtaker_bankability
Where pre-sales, pre-lets or off-taker commitments support financing, distinguish demand evidence from
usable cash. Reconcile qualifying commitments to the lender definition and model deposits/advance receipts
according to refundability, escrow/trust restrictions, release conditions, cancellation/default and final
settlement treatment.

### T2-S10-260 — test: property_landowner_jv_land_consideration
Where land is contributed by a landowner/JV partner or paid on deferred/participating terms, reconcile the
reduced upfront land cash with the actual consideration: deferred price, units, sale proceeds, preferred return,
profit share or equity interest. Confirm the land contribution is neither omitted as 'free' nor double counted.

### T2-S10-261 — test: property_construction_drawdown_progress_conditions
Where development debt is drawn against project progress, reconcile debt availability/draw timing with the
contractual property conditions such as certified works, eligible cost, equity-first requirements, cost-to-
complete tests or lender-monitor approval. Do not assume the entire committed facility is usable from day one
unless the actual terms permit it.

### T2-S10-262 — test: property_financing_timeline_commitment_alignment
Compare financing approval, financial close and satisfaction of conditions precedent with land settlement,
construction commitments and other unconditional project obligations. Challenge a funding case in which the
developer becomes committed before sufficient bridge/equity/closed financing exists to meet those property
obligations.

### T2-S10-263 — test: property_local_capital_market_availability
Where the model relies on a specific funding channel — bank development debt, mezzanine, REIT/equity,
bond, public-sector support, high-net-worth/private capital or another instrument — test whether there is
transaction-specific evidence that the source is available for the jurisdiction, asset class, sponsor, size and
stage. Do not use country survey rankings or another market's financing mix as a universal availability rule.

### T2-S10-264 — test: property_cycle_supply_credit_lead_lag
Where a model spans a material development/holding period, test whether occupier demand, vacancy, rent, yields,
competing supply and development timing form a coherent property-cycle view. Challenge a model that extrapolates
strong current rents/low vacancy through delivery of new competing supply without considering the development lag.

### T2-S10-265 — test: property_refinancing_market_cycle_joint_stress
Where refinance, extension or future debt availability is material, test the property-specific joint downside of weaker
NOI, lower collateral value/higher cap rate and tighter financing availability. Do not assume current leverage, spread or
lender appetite automatically persists to the refinance date.

### T2-S10-266 — test: property_securitisation_underlying_collateral_risk
For a property/loan exposed to CMBS or another mortgage-backed structure, reconcile debt service and refinance risk to
underlying tenant income, vacancy, NOI, collateral value, maturity structure and applicable tranche/payment priority.
Confirm securitisation redistributes rather than eliminates the property risk.

### T2-S10-267 — test: property_balloon_extension_refinance_risk
Where commercial property debt leaves material principal due at maturity, test whether repayment depends on sale,
refinancing or another evidenced source. Assess extension risk where expected refinance proceeds may be unavailable
because property income, value or credit-market conditions have weakened.

### T2-S10-268 — test: property_reit_market_price_nav_separation
Where a listed property/REIT model uses both public-market value and direct-property NAV, keep property-level value,
vehicle NAV and listed equity price separate. Confirm any premium/discount to NAV is not double counted as property
cash flow or treated as an automatic correction to the underlying appraisal.

### T2-S10-269 — test: property_crossborder_currency_return_basis
For cross-border property investment, reconcile local-currency property cash flows, debt currency, hedge mechanics and
translation/repatriation to the investor reporting currency. Do not claim diversification or home-currency return from
local property returns without considering exchange-rate and market/institutional effects.

### T2-S10-270 — test: property_standards_currency_and_status
Where a model or valuation claims compliance/alignment with a property standard, identify the operative
edition for the valuation date, jurisdiction, purpose and practitioner. Do not treat the 2026 IVS Exposure
Draft, RICS consultation drafts or archived editions as binding current rules; apply local/national authority
where it supersedes or supplements global guidance.

### T2-S10-271 — test: property_value_uncertainty_and_false_precision
Where property evidence is thin, volatile or the valuation is highly sensitive, identify the principal sources
of value uncertainty and test whether the precision/range presented is economically supportable. Keep genuine
value uncertainty separate from actual modelling error; do not manufacture statistical confidence without a
property-market basis.

### T2-S10-272 — test: property_land_area_basis_and_density
Reconcile legal ownership/title area, planning site area and net development area where they differ. Confirm
density, land-value rates, infrastructure allocations, coverage and GDV use the correct denominator and that
excluded/non-developable land does not acquire saleable/buildable economics.

### T2-S10-273 — test: property_dcf_forecast_period_and_terminal_reliance
For an explicit property DCF, test whether the forecast horizon is linked to lease events, stabilisation,
refurbishment/redevelopment or another property rationale. Quantify/review terminal-value reliance and
reconcile exit value to forward NOI/rent, vacancy, capex, condition, remaining life and the exit yield at the
terminal date.

### T2-S10-274 — test: property_market_growth_asset_depreciation_bridge
Where rents or values use market/location growth, test whether asset-specific physical, functional and economic
obsolescence is also reflected where material. Reconcile refurbishment/repositioning with any reset in relative
rent, occupancy, useful life or exit yield; do not apply a universal annual depreciation rate.

### T2-S10-275 — test: property_development_cost_prediction_basis
Reconcile major cost rates/allowances to the scheme scope, area/measurement basis, design maturity,
specification, location, procurement basis and price/base date. Confirm material development cost categories
are classified consistently and required costs do not disappear through gross/net area or taxonomy changes.

### T2-S10-276 — test: property_cost_to_complete_current_basis
For a scheme in progress, rebuild the economic cost to complete from completed/paid work, commitments,
remaining works, variations/claims, known defects, remaining contingency, retentions/warranties and timing.
Do not accept original budget less spend as cost to complete when current project information has changed.

### T2-S10-277 — test: property_entitlement_stage_optionality
Where planning/entitlement risk is material, separate land/rights acquisition, entitlement advancement and
irreversible construction commitment. Test whether the model preserves or destroys genuine wait/redesign/
phase/sell options and whether holding costs, approval expiry, tenure/option rights and other constraints make
waiting physically and legally exercisable.

### T2-S10-278 — test: property_development_in_progress_value_bridge
For a partially completed scheme, reconcile the modelled value to the exact stage reached, remaining time/cost,
contractor/professional arrangements, claims/variations, warranties/bonds, outstanding consent conditions,
pre-commitments and remobilisation/deterioration risk. Do not use completed GDV less unspent budget as an
unsupported proxy for work-in-progress value.

### T2-S10-279 — test: property_residual_output_land_vs_developer_return
Identify what the residual appraisal solves for. If land value is the output, reconcile GDV, all development/
finance costs and required developer return; if land cost is known, ensure developer profit/IRR is the output.
Confirm target profit/return is not also buried as a duplicate cost allowance.

### T2-S10-280 — test: property_service_charge_recovery_timing_and_funding
For recoverable property costs, reconcile lease/service-charge eligibility, allocation, caps/floors, vacancy
leakage and recovery timing. Distinguish retained balances, forward funding, deferred recovery/payment plans
and landlord bridge funding; do not treat ultimate recoverability as same-period cash receipt.

### T2-S10-281 — test: property_technical_due_diligence_cashflow_bridge
For material technical/site-condition findings, trace each relevant issue into the property economics — repair/
compliance capex, lifecycle spend, downtime, operating cost, useful life, marketability, insurance/finance or
redevelopment value. Do not add generic engineering allowances unrelated to the actual asset.

### T2-S10-282 — test: property_refurbishment_repositioning_value_bridge
Where refurbishment/repositioning capex is material, reconcile spend and timing to the rent, occupancy,
incentives, operating efficiency, economic life and/or exit yield benefits claimed. Test the converse case:
removing or delaying the works should not leave post-refurbishment performance unchanged without evidence.

### T2-S10-283 — test: property_sustainability_energy_value_pathway
Where sustainability, energy performance or regulation is material to market participants, model the specific
property pathways supported by evidence — retrofit/compliance capex, operating cost, rent/incentives,
occupancy, legal lettability/use, financing/insurance or exit liquidity/value. Do not impose a universal green
premium, brown discount or ESG haircut.

### T2-S10-284 — test: property_climate_physical_risk_transmission
Where flood, heat, fire, storm, water stress or another physical hazard is material, reconcile the evidenced
exposure to damage/mitigation capex, downtime, insurance, operating cost, tenant demand, financeability and
exit marketability/value as applicable. Avoid double counting the same risk in cash flows and an unsupported
yield/discount-rate adjustment.
